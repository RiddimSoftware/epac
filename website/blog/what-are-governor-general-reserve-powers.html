What are the Governor General's Reserve Powers?

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

The Governor General of Canada acts almost entirely on the advice of the Prime Minister. But not always. The GG holds a set of reserve powers — theoretical constitutional tools to act independently in cases where doing so is necessary to protect parliamentary democracy. Here is what they are and the one famous time they were tested.

The Normal Rule: Act on Advice

By constitutional convention, the GG almost always acts on the advice of the PM. When the PM says dissolve Parliament, the GG dissolves it. When the PM asks for prorogation, the GG grants it. When cabinet presents an Order in Council, the GG signs it. The GG's role is largely ceremonial — a formal checkpoint rather than an independent decision-maker.

When Reserve Powers Apply

The reserve powers are the exception: the GG can in theory refuse a request from the PM if it would clearly violate the constitution or undermine fundamental democratic principles. Scholars identify three main situations:

  • Refusing dissolution after an election — If a PM who just lost an election requests immediate dissolution before allowing the opposition to attempt to form government, the GG could theoretically refuse and invite the leader of the opposition to try.
  • Dismissing a government — Historically possible but virtually inconceivable in modern Canada.
  • Refusing Royal Assent — Theoretically possible; has not happened in federal Canada since 1945.

The King-Byng Affair, 1926

The most significant Canadian example of reserve powers in action is the King-Byng Affair of 1926. Prime Minister Mackenzie King, facing a confidence vote he expected to lose, asked Governor General Lord Byng to dissolve Parliament. Byng refused — arguing it was not right to dissolve so soon after the last election — and instead invited the Conservative leader Meighen to form government. Meighen's government lasted only days before losing a confidence vote. The subsequent election became a referendum on Byng's decision; King won, and the precedent shifted toward almost unconditional acceptance of PM advice.

The 2008 Prorogation

In 2008, Prime Minister Harper requested prorogation to avoid a non-confidence vote. Governor General Michaëlle Jean granted the request after 2.5 hours of deliberation — but not without making clear that it was not automatic. Constitutional scholars debated whether Jean should have refused, but she chose to accept the advice, setting a modern precedent for leniency.
