What is a Private Member's Bill?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Not every law in Canada comes from the Prime Minister's office. Individual Members of Parliament — including opposition MPs — can introduce their own legislation through Private Member's Bills (PMBs). Understanding how these work helps explain a significant part of what happens in the House of Commons every session.

Government Bills vs. Private Member's Bills

The Canadian Parliament has two main types of legislation:

  • Government bills (C-1 through C-200 by convention) — introduced by cabinet ministers on behalf of the government. These carry the full weight of the ruling party and are expected to pass if the government has a majority.
  • Private Member's Bills (also numbered C-200 and up, or historically by ballot) — introduced by any MP who is not a cabinet minister. These can come from any party, including government backbenchers.

Senate bills follow the same logic: government Senate bills versus Private Senators' Bills.

How Does an MP Get a PMB Considered?

At the start of each Parliament, all MPs who want to introduce Private Member's Business enter a lottery. The order in which they can bring forward their bills is randomly determined. This means an opposition MP drawn early in the lottery can get their bill debated before a government backbencher drawn later — regardless of party.

Each bill gets a limited amount of debate time (typically three hours at second reading, spread across several sitting days). After that, the House votes on whether to continue. Most PMBs die here — the government rarely whips its caucus to support opposition bills, and scheduling is constrained.

Do Private Member's Bills Ever Pass?

Yes — and more often than most people realize. Famous examples include bills on parental leave, criminal sentencing provisions, and heritage designations that started as Private Member's Bills. When the government is in a minority position, PMBs have an even higher chance of passing because the opposition parties can combine their votes.

The key signal to watch: whether the government applies a three-line whip (requiring all government MPs to vote the same way) or allows a free vote (where government MPs can vote their conscience). Free votes significantly raise a PMB's odds of passage.
