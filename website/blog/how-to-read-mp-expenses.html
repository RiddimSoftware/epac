How to Read an MP's Expense Report

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Every Member of Parliament is required to publicly disclose their spending on travel, hospitality, and contracts every quarter. These disclosures are published on the Parliament of Canada website and are available to any Canadian. The problem is that the raw reports are spreadsheets buried in a government portal that few people know how to navigate. Here is a plain-language guide to what the numbers mean.

The Three Categories

MP proactive disclosures are broken into three types of spending:

✈️ Travel

All travel claims for the MP and their staff — flights, hotels, ground transportation, and meals on official business. Each claim shows the traveller's name, purpose of travel, departure and destination points, dates, and total cost. The biggest travel expense is typically constituency-Ottawa travel: MPs fly home most weekends the House is sitting.

🍽️ Hospitality

Expenses for meals and events with external guests on parliamentary business. Each entry shows the date, location, number of attendees, purpose of the event, and total. Common entries include meals with constituents, stakeholder meetings, and events in the riding. Note: hospitality is defined narrowly — a lunch with a fellow MP doesn't qualify, but a lunch with a community group does.

📄 Contracts

Contracts with outside suppliers for goods or services over $200. These are typically for constituency office expenses: web design, printing, event services, IT support. Each entry shows the supplier name, contract description, date, and total amount.

What the Totals Mean

The summary row shows four totals for the quarter: salaries, travel, hospitality, and contracts. Salaries cover the MP's own salary (which is fixed by law and the same for all backbenchers) plus their staff's salaries — these are not particularly meaningful for comparison since most offices have similar headcounts.

Travel, hospitality, and contracts are the most informative. An MP with an unusually high travel number might represent a remote riding where flights are expensive, or might be travelling frequently for committee work. An MP with zero hospitality in a quarter might simply have had a quieter constituency calendar — not necessarily a red flag.

Common Questions

Why are some numbers surprisingly small? The disclosure threshold for contracts is $200. Anything below that doesn't appear. And MPs who use primarily staff time for work rather than outside contractors will have low contract totals regardless of how active their office is.

Why is travel so high? Ottawa is a long way from most ridings. An MP from British Columbia might spend $3,000–$5,000 per quarter just on return flights. This is a structural feature of a geographically large country, not evidence of waste.

What should I look for? Compare the same MP quarter-over-quarter rather than MP-to-MP. Sudden spikes in contracting or hospitality outside of an election year are worth a second look. The supplier names on contracts occasionally reveal connections worth noting.
