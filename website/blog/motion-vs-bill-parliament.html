Motion vs. Bill: What's the Difference?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

When you read Hansard or follow votes in epac, you will often see both "bills" and "motions" voted on by MPs. They look similar — someone moves something, the House debates, a vote is called — but they have very different effects. Understanding the distinction helps you interpret what Parliament is actually deciding.

Bills: Creating and Changing Law

A bill is a proposal to create, amend, or repeal legislation. If passed by both the House and Senate and given Royal Assent, it becomes an Act of Parliament — a law with binding legal force. Bills go through multiple readings, committee study, and both chambers. The process is deliberate and time-consuming precisely because laws are hard to reverse.

Bills are numbered: government bills in the House start at C-2 (C-1 is reserved for the pro forma first bill of each session), Private Members' Bills at C-201, and Senate bills begin with S-.

Motions: Expressing the Will of the House

A motion is a proposal for the House to make a decision or express an opinion. Most motions, if passed, do not create law — they direct the government to take a certain action, express the sense of Parliament, or grant the House permission to do something procedural. Common examples:

  • Supply motions (the main exception — these authorize government spending and are legally binding)
  • Address in Reply to the Speech from the Throne — the House's formal response to the government's agenda
  • Opposition Day motions — non-binding expressions of the opposition's position on policy
  • Procedural motions — time allocation, adjournment, referrals to committee

The Key Practical Difference

Voting against a bill is voting to prevent a law from passing. Voting against a motion is voting to prevent the House from expressing a particular opinion or direction. Unless a motion is a confidence matter or a supply motion, defeating it does not typically change what the government can legally do — it just signals political opposition.

This is why opposition parties sometimes pass motions that the government then ignores: a non-binding motion expressing "regret" about a policy does not compel the government to change that policy.
