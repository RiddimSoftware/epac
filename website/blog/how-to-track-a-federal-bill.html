How to Track a Federal Bill in Canada

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Every law in Canada starts as a bill. Between introduction and Royal Assent, a bill can travel through dozens of readings, committee hearings, amendments, and votes — a process that can take months or years. Here is how the system works, and how to follow a bill you care about without losing track.

The Stages of a Federal Bill

Federal bills can originate in either the House of Commons or the Senate. Most government bills start in the House. Here is what each stage means:

  1. 1
    First Reading — The bill is introduced and tabled. No debate. The title and basic description are recorded in Hansard. This is when the bill gets its number (e.g. C-50 for a House bill, S-12 for a Senate bill).
  2. 2
    Second Reading — The House debates the principle of the bill. This is the big public debate — speeches, question period references, media coverage. The vote at this stage is on whether the bill should proceed at all.
  3. 3
    Committee Study — The bill is sent to the appropriate standing committee (Finance, Justice, Health, etc.) for clause-by-clause review. Witnesses are called, amendments proposed. Committee hearings are where the most substantive work happens — often away from cameras.
  4. 4
    Report Stage — The House reviews the committee's amendments. Members can propose further changes. Another vote.
  5. 5
    Third Reading — Final House debate and vote on the full bill as amended. If it passes, it moves to the Senate.
  6. 6
    Senate — The Senate repeats a similar process (first, second, third reading plus committee study). The Senate can pass, amend, or defeat the bill.
  7. 7
    Royal Assent — The Governor General (or their deputy) formally approves the bill, making it law. Royal Assent is typically a brief ceremony; the date becomes the bill's official passage date.

Where to Find Official Bill Status

The Parliament of Canada publishes bill status through LEGISinfo (parl.ca), which tracks every bill's current stage, all recorded votes on it, and links to the full text. The site is comprehensive but not designed for casual readers — navigating it requires some familiarity with parliamentary procedure.

The Commissioner of Lobbying publishes records of who is lobbying on which bills, a dimension LEGISinfo doesn't cover.

What to Watch For

The most consequential moments in a bill's life are usually not the headline votes. Committee study is where bills get amended, gutted, or strengthened — often without much media coverage. epac's committee feature lets you follow the committee hearings that matter, including witness testimony and clause-by-clause reviews.

The recorded votes at Second Reading and Third Reading are the two moments to watch for party-line signals. If MPs vote against their party on a government bill, that is a meaningful signal worth noting.
