What Are the Standing Orders of Parliament?

April 27, 2026 · 3 min read

When you read Hansard and see references to "Standing Order 31" or procedural exchanges about "the Standing Orders," you are seeing Parliament's internal rulebook at work. The Standing Orders are the formal rules that govern how the House of Commons conducts its business — from how long an MP can speak to how committees are structured.

What the Standing Orders Cover

The Standing Orders are a comprehensive document covering virtually every aspect of House procedure:

  • How and when the House sits (daily schedule, sitting hours)
  • Time limits on speeches at each stage of debate
  • How bills move through their readings
  • Committee composition, powers, and reporting obligations
  • Question Period rules (45 minutes, alternating between parties)
  • Private Members' Business rules (the ballot system)
  • Financial procedure and supply days

Standing Order 31, specifically, is what MPs use for "Member's Statements" — the brief 60-second statements MPs can make at the start of each sitting before Question Period. When you see Hansard entries labelled "S.O. 31 (Statements by Members)," that is the rule being applied.

How the Standing Orders Can Change

The Standing Orders are controlled entirely by the House itself. They can be changed by a motion of the House — which can be moved by any party. Changes to procedural rules often require all-party agreement in practice, because the opposition is unlikely to agree to changes that weaken their ability to scrutinize the government. Significant rule changes are sometimes negotiated through the Standing Committee on Procedure and House Affairs.

The government can also make temporary changes for specific bills using time allocation or special orders, but these do not permanently alter the Standing Orders.
