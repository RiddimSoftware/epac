What is a Non-Confidence Vote?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

A non-confidence vote is one of the most consequential events in Canadian parliamentary democracy. When the House of Commons passes a motion of non-confidence in the government, the Prime Minister must either resign, call an election, or request that the Governor General dissolve Parliament. Here is how it works.

The Confidence Convention

Canada's parliamentary system operates on the principle of responsible government: the executive (cabinet, led by the Prime Minister) must maintain the confidence of the elected legislature (the House of Commons). If the House withdraws that confidence, the government falls.

This convention traces to British parliamentary tradition and is not written into Canada's constitution — it is a constitutional convention, as binding in practice as any written law.

What Counts as a Confidence Vote?

Not every vote is a confidence matter. Three categories automatically constitute confidence votes:

  • Explicit non-confidence motions — a motion worded "This House has no confidence in the government" or similar. Any MP can introduce one.
  • The budget (Ways and Means) — the government's annual budget is traditionally a confidence matter. Defeating the budget means defeating the government.
  • The Speech from the Throne — the address that opens each parliamentary session is also a confidence measure; defeating it topples the government.

Other votes can be designated as confidence matters by the government (making them "whipped" votes where all government MPs must support). Ordinary bills, amendments, and committee reports are generally not confidence matters unless the government explicitly says so.

What Happens When Confidence is Lost?

When a confidence vote fails, the Prime Minister has two options: resign (allowing the Governor General to ask another party leader to form government if the math supports it) or ask the Governor General to dissolve Parliament and call an election. In practice, a defeated government almost always requests dissolution and an election follows.

In minority government situations — where no single party holds a majority of House seats — confidence votes happen more frequently and carry genuine uncertainty. A minority government can fall at any time if opposition parties combine their votes.
