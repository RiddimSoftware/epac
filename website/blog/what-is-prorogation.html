What is Prorogation?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Prorogation is one of the least understood procedural tools in Canadian parliamentary democracy — and one of the most consequential. When Parliament is prorogued, the current session ends, all bills die on the order paper, and MPs go home. But Parliament is not dissolved, and no election is called. Here is what it means and why it matters.

What Prorogation Does

Prorogation ends a parliamentary session. It is formally requested by the Prime Minister and granted by the Governor General. The effects are:

  • All unfinished business dies. Bills that have not received Royal Assent — regardless of how far they have progressed — expire. If the government wants to continue with them, it must reintroduce them from scratch in the new session.
  • Parliament stops sitting. No debates, no question period, no committees, no votes until the new session opens with a new Speech from the Throne.
  • No election is called. MPs keep their seats. The government remains in place. It is a pause, not an ending.

Normal vs. Controversial Uses

Routine prorogation — ending a session after the government has completed most of its legislative agenda — is a normal part of parliamentary life. Most Parliaments have multiple sessions.

Prorogation becomes controversial when it appears to be used to avoid accountability. The most significant recent example in Canada was in 2008, when Prime Minister Harper prorogued Parliament to prevent the passage of a non-confidence motion when a coalition of opposition parties threatened to topple the government. In 2020, Prime Minister Trudeau prorogued Parliament as it was studying cabinet conduct related to the WE Charity controversy. Both prorogations were criticized as misuses of the tool to shield the government from scrutiny.
