What Does the Canadian Senate Actually Do?

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

The Senate of Canada is the upper chamber of Parliament — but unlike the elected House of Commons, its 105 members are appointed. This creates the central tension around the Senate: it has real power to delay and amend legislation, yet derives none of its legitimacy from democratic election. Here is what the Senate actually does in practice.

Reviewing and Amending Bills

The Senate's primary legislative role is reviewing bills that have passed the House of Commons. Senators read bills at committee, hear from expert witnesses, and can propose amendments. When the Senate amends a bill, it goes back to the House for concurrence — the House must accept, reject, or negotiate the changes. In recent sessions, the Senate has sent back significant amendments on a number of bills, forcing substantive changes to government legislation.

Senate Committee Work

Senate committees are often where the Senate's most substantive work happens. Because senators serve until 75 and are less constrained by electoral cycles, Senate committees can conduct long-form studies — sometimes spanning multiple sessions — on complex policy areas. Studies on Indigenous reconciliation, privacy law, defence policy, and mental health have produced influential reports that shape government priorities even when they don't produce legislation.

Senate Bills

The Senate can also introduce its own bills. Government bills in the Senate (starting with S-) are typically introduced first in the Senate for procedural reasons before going to the House. Independent senators can also introduce private senators' bills on their own initiative. These follow the same process as House bills: they must pass both chambers and receive Royal Assent to become law.

The Independent Senate Model

Since 2016, the Senate has shifted toward a more independent model, with the largest caucus being the Independent Senators Group (ISG). Independent senators are expected to vote based on their own judgment rather than a party line, making Senate votes harder to predict and the chamber more genuinely deliberative than it was in earlier decades when partisan caucuses dominated.
