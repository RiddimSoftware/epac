What is a Filibuster in Canadian Parliament?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

A filibuster is a deliberate tactic to delay or prevent legislation from passing by extending debate far beyond what the subject requires. Canada does not have the same form of unlimited Senate filibuster famous in the United States, but the House of Commons and its committees have their own version — and it gets used.

How It Works in the House of Commons

Under the Standing Orders, debate time is limited for most stages of a bill. MPs cannot simply speak indefinitely on the floor. But the rules provide several tools for delay:

  • Extended speeches — MPs can speak for up to 20 minutes per turn during debate, and with 338 members and multiple readings, this adds up
  • Quorum calls — An MP can call for a quorum check; if fewer than 20 members are present, the House must suspend
  • Bells — Before a recorded vote, bells ring for a set period (typically 30 minutes, but up to 1 hour for some votes); repeated procedural votes extend the session
  • Committee obstruction — Filibusters most commonly occur in committee, where opposition members can speak at length on clause-by-clause review, preventing completion

How the Government Responds

When a filibuster is underway, the government has tools to respond. Time allocation motions limit the total hours of debate on a bill at each stage. Closure is a stronger tool that immediately ends debate and forces a vote. Both are controversial — the opposition views them as silencing legitimate scrutiny; the government views them as necessary to advance its legislative agenda.

What You See in Hansard

Filibuster debates appear in Hansard as extremely long transcripts with many speakers and, in committee transcripts, repetitive procedural exchanges.
