What is Royal Assent?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

When you see a bill marked "Royal Assent" in epac, it means that bill is now law. Royal Assent is the final constitutional step in the Canadian legislative process — the moment a bill approved by both Parliament and the Senate officially becomes an Act of Parliament. Here is what it involves and why it matters.

The Role of the Governor General

Canada is a constitutional monarchy. Formally, all legislation requires the approval of the Crown — represented federally by the Governor General, or by provincial Lieutenant Governors for provincial bills. In practice, the Crown's approval is a formality: by constitutional convention, the Governor General always grants Royal Assent when asked by the government. The last time Royal Assent was withheld in Canada was 1945, and that refusal was overturned.

Royal Assent is typically given at a ceremony in the Senate chamber, attended by both MPs and senators. The Governor General (or more often, a deputy) pronounces short formulas in French and English for each bill. Since 2002, most bills receive Royal Assent by written declaration rather than a ceremony.

Royal Assent vs. Coming Into Force

This distinction trips up many people. Royal Assent makes a bill law, but it does not necessarily make it operative. Many bills include provisions that come into force "on a day to be fixed by proclamation" — meaning the government decides when the specific sections actually take effect. A bill can receive Royal Assent in June and not come into force until a regulation is passed a year later.

Some bills include delayed commencement dates — "this Act comes into force six months after Royal Assent." Others come into force immediately. When following a bill in epac, Royal Assent means the legislative battle is over; the implementation battle may be just beginning.

What the Date Means

The Royal Assent date shown in epac is the official date the bill became law. For historical research, this date matters because it establishes when the legal obligations, rights, or programs in the bill were created — even if they weren't operative until later. Courts use the Royal Assent date, not the coming-into-force date, for certain legal purposes.
