Canada's Senate vs the House of Commons

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Canada's Parliament has two chambers: the elected House of Commons and the appointed Senate. Most Canadians know the House — it's where MPs debate, vote, and hold the government to account. The Senate is less visible but plays a significant role in how laws are made. Here is how the two chambers differ and why both matter.

The Core Difference: Elected vs. Appointed

Members of the House of Commons are elected by Canadians in federal ridings. There are 338 ridings across Canada, and each elects one MP. Elections happen at least every five years, and the party that wins the most seats typically forms the government.

Senators are appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister. There are 105 senators, distributed roughly by province and region rather than by population. Senators serve until age 75. Because they are appointed — not elected — they are meant to be insulated from short-term political pressures and able to scrutinize legislation independently.

What Does the Senate Actually Do?

The Senate's official role is sometimes called "sober second thought" — a deliberate review of legislation that has already passed the House. For a bill to become law, it must pass both chambers and receive Royal Assent. That means the Senate can:

  • Pass a bill without changes (most common outcome)
  • Amend a bill and send it back to the House for concurrence
  • Reject a bill outright (rare, but it happens)
  • Introduce and study their own bills (Senate public bills)

In practice, the Senate has significantly revised government legislation on issues like cannabis legalization, online streaming, and national security. When the Senate amends a bill, the House must vote on whether to accept, reject, or negotiate the changes — a process that can add weeks or months to a bill's timeline.

How Senate Independence Has Evolved

Since 2016, successive governments have moved toward an "independent" Senate appointment process. Senators are now encouraged to sit as independents rather than under a party whip. The largest caucus is currently the Independent Senators Group (ISG), which operates without formal party affiliation. Conservative senators still sit as a formal caucus (CSG). This shift has made Senate votes less predictable and more genuinely deliberative than in earlier decades.
