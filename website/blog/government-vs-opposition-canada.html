Government vs. Opposition: How Canadian Parliament Works

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

In the House of Commons, MPs physically sit on two sides of the chamber. The government sits to the Speaker's right; the opposition sits to the left. This seating arrangement is not just tradition — it reflects a fundamental principle of parliamentary democracy: the executive must answer to those who oppose it.

The Government Side

The "government" in parliamentary terms means the cabinet and its supporting caucus — the MPs who belong to the party (or coalition) that holds the confidence of the House. The Prime Minister sits in the front row, flanked by cabinet ministers. Government MPs are expected to vote with cabinet on confidence matters; on other votes, the party whip's guidance varies.

The government controls the agenda of the House: which bills are introduced, when they are debated, and how much time is allocated. Government bills have priority over private members' bills and opposition motions.

The Official Opposition

The party with the second-largest number of seats forms the Official Opposition. Its leader is the Leader of the Opposition — a formal constitutional role that receives a salary, an office budget, and security protection comparable to a minister's. The Official Opposition has the first opportunity to question the government in Question Period and leads the response to the Speech from the Throne.

The Official Opposition's job is not simply to oppose everything: it is to hold the government accountable, offer alternative policies, scrutinize legislation, and serve as the credible alternative government-in-waiting.

Third Parties and Independents

Parties with fewer seats than the Official Opposition are "third parties." They have fewer resources, less speaking time, and no automatic right to lead the first response in debates. Independent MPs — those not affiliated with any recognized party — have the fewest procedural privileges but can still speak, vote, and introduce private members' bills.
