What are Orders in Council?

April 27, 2026 · 3 min read

Not everything the federal government does requires an act of Parliament. Many government decisions — appointing senators, setting regulations, designating holidays — are made through Orders in Council (OICs). Here is what they are, how they work, and why they matter for democratic accountability.

What an Order in Council Is

An Order in Council is a formal decision made by the Governor in Council — the Governor General acting on the advice of the federal cabinet. In practice, this means cabinet approves the measure and the Governor General signs it into effect. No debate, no vote, no House of Commons involvement.

OICs are used for many purposes:

  • Appointments (senators, ambassadors, judges of superior courts, heads of Crown corporations)
  • Regulations under existing legislation (the details of how a law operates)
  • Bringing laws into force (many acts say "comes into force on a day fixed by OIC")
  • Declaring emergencies under the Emergencies Act
  • Intergovernmental agreements

Why They Matter for Accountability

Because OICs bypass Parliament, they are sometimes controversial. When a government uses regulations under existing law to implement significant policy changes — without passing new legislation — opposition parties argue this avoids democratic scrutiny. OICs are also used for controversial appointments that opponents argue bypass merit-based processes.

All OICs are published in the Canada Gazette — the official government journal — so they are technically public. But they receive far less attention than legislation passing through Parliament.
