What is Unanimous Consent in Parliament?

April 27, 2026 · 3 min read

Parliament's rules are designed for deliberation — debate takes time, votes require bells, motions must be tabled in advance. But sometimes the House needs to act quickly, or all parties simply agree on something minor. Unanimous consent is the mechanism that lets Parliament bypass its own rules, instantly, when every MP agrees.

How it Works

Any MP can ask for unanimous consent for almost anything: to skip a procedural step, to take note of an anniversary, to introduce a bill without the usual notice, to change the speaking order for the day. The Speaker asks: "Is there unanimous consent?" If no one objects, it is granted. The entire exchange typically takes under ten seconds in Hansard.

If even one MP calls out "No" or rises to object, unanimous consent is denied. That single MP has effectively blocked the entire House, at least on that specific request. This veto power is rarely exercised maliciously, but it does happen — particularly when a motion would benefit a political opponent or when an MP disagrees with the framing of a commemorative statement.

What You See in Hansard

Unanimous consent requests appear in Hansard as brief exchanges: the request, the Speaker's question, and either "Agreed" (unanimous consent granted) or a note of dissent. They are common enough to appear multiple times per sitting day, often at the start or end of proceedings.
