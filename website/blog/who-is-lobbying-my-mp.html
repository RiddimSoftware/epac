Who is Lobbying Your MP?

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Every year, hundreds of organizations pay professional lobbyists to meet with Members of Parliament, ministers, and senior government officials. These meetings are a normal and legal part of democratic governance — but most Canadians have no idea they're happening or what's being discussed. Canada's lobbying transparency laws require that every such communication be registered publicly. Here is how to read that registry and what to look for.

What is the Commissioner of Lobbying?

The Commissioner of Lobbying is an independent officer of Parliament responsible for administering the Lobbying Act. The Commissioner maintains a public registry of all registered lobbyists and their communications with government officials. The registry is searchable at lobbycanada.gc.ca and contains records going back more than a decade.

The Act requires registration when a paid lobbyist communicates with a "public office holder" — which includes MPs, senators, ministers, deputy ministers, and their staff — about any of the following:

  • The development of legislation or regulations
  • Government contracts or grants
  • Government policy, program, or directive
  • Anything affecting an organization's financial interests

What Gets Recorded

Each registered communication includes:

  • The lobbyist's employer — which organization are they representing? This could be a corporation, an industry association, an NGO, a union, or a university.
  • The subject matter — a category (e.g. "Health," "Taxation," "Environment") and a brief description of the issue being lobbied on
  • The public office holder — which MP, minister, or official did they meet with?
  • The date of the communication
  • The method — oral, written, or both

What you won't find: the content of the meeting itself. Lobbyists are required to disclose that a meeting happened and its general subject, but not what was said. Think of it as the meeting log, not the minutes.

What This Tells You

Lobbying disclosure answers one key question: who has had private access to your MP?

When an MP speaks in favour of a particular industry's position in a committee hearing or in the House, cross-referencing their lobbying records shows whether representatives of that industry met with them beforehand. This is not evidence of wrongdoing — lobbying is legal and often legitimate — but it's context that every engaged voter should have access to.

Look especially for patterns: an MP who receives multiple contacts from the same organization over several quarters, or who receives contacts from industry groups right before a relevant vote, deserves a closer look.

How to Find Lobbying Records in epac

In epac, open any MP's profile and scroll to the Lobbyist Connections section. This shows the most recent communications from registered lobbyists, with the organization name, subject matter, and date. Tap "See All" for the full list with a link directly to the Commissioner's registry entry for each organization.

The data in epac comes directly from the Commissioner of Lobbying's public registry — the same data that journalists and researchers use, now available without navigating a government portal.
