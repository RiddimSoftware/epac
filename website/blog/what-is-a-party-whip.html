What is a Party Whip?

April 27, 2026 · 3 min read

Every party in the House of Commons has a party whip — an MP whose job is to ensure that their caucus shows up for votes and, when required, votes the way the party leadership has directed. The role is more nuanced than simple enforcement, and it has direct relevance to how you read MP voting records in epac.

What the Whip Does

The party whip is responsible for:

  • Communicating the party position on upcoming votes to all caucus members
  • Tracking which MPs are present and which have conflicts or travel obligations
  • Arranging paired votes when an MP cannot attend
  • Enforcing party discipline — in extreme cases, recommending removal from caucus

Paired Votes

When an MP cannot attend a vote, the whip can arrange a "pairing" — an agreement with the opposing side where one government and one opposition MP both agree not to vote, cancelling each other out. This is a courtesy arrangement that has no formal legal status and has become less common in recent years. When you see "Paired" in an MP's voting record in epac, this is what it means.

Free Votes

On some issues — typically matters of conscience like abortion, end-of-life care, or certain private members' bills — parties allow a "free vote" where MPs vote according to their own judgment rather than the party line. In a free vote, the whip does not direct the caucus. This is why you sometimes see significant divergence within a party on specific bills; it does not always mean rebellion.

What This Means for Party Solidarity Scores

The party solidarity score in epac measures how often an MP votes with the majority of their caucus. A lower score on free-vote issues is expected and not evidence of independence from the party on policy grounds. The most meaningful departures from the party line are on confidence votes and whipped government legislation — where divergence has real consequences.
