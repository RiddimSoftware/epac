Minority vs. Majority Government in Canada

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Canadian federal elections produce either a majority or a minority government depending on how many seats the winning party wins. The difference has enormous practical implications for how Parliament functions and how long the government survives.

The Threshold: 170 Seats

There are 338 seats in the House of Commons. A party needs 170 or more — a bare majority — to form a majority government. With 170+ seats, a government can pass legislation and survive confidence votes without any support from other parties.

If no party reaches 170, the party with the most seats typically forms a minority government. It can govern only as long as it retains the confidence of the House — meaning it must either win enough votes from opposition MPs on key legislation, or avoid losing confidence votes altogether.

How Minority Governments Survive

A minority government has several strategies:

  • Case-by-case negotiation — Work with different opposition parties on different bills, building temporary majorities without a formal agreement
  • Confidence and supply agreements — A formal arrangement where a smaller party agrees to support the government on confidence votes (and supply) in exchange for policy commitments, without joining the cabinet. The 2022 Liberal-NDP agreement is a recent example.
  • Coalition government — Multiple parties share cabinet posts and govern jointly. True coalitions are rare in Canadian federal history but common in provinces.

Why Minority Governments Fall

A minority government falls when it loses a confidence vote. This can happen intentionally — when the government introduces a bill knowing it will lose, to trigger an election on favourable terrain — or accidentally, when expected support fails to materialize. The timing of elections matters: a minority government that survives long enough can sometimes rebuild enough support for a subsequent majority.

What This Means for Following Parliament

Minority governments change the legislative calculus significantly. In a minority situation, opposition amendments are more likely to succeed, opposition bills have a higher chance of passing, and the government must consult more broadly.
