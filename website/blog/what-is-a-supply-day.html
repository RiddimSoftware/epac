What is a Supply Day?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

In Parliament, most of the legislative calendar is controlled by the government. It decides which bills are debated, when, and for how long. Supply days — also called opposition days — are the exception: a set number of days each year where opposition parties get to choose the topic and motion for debate.

How Supply Days Work

Supply days are formally part of the supply process — the parliamentary procedure for approving government spending. In practice, their real significance is giving opposition parties uninterrupted floor time. There are 22 supply days per Parliament divided roughly proportionally among opposition parties. The party given a supply day chooses a motion, and the House debates it for that full sitting day.

Unlike Question Period, where the government can deflect and redirect, supply day motions let the opposition set the agenda, frame the issue, and force ministers to defend their positions on opposition terms. The motions are usually non-binding, but the debates become part of the public record in Hansard.

What Makes a Supply Day Significant

Supply day votes can be — rarely — used as vehicles for confidence motions. An opposition party can word its supply day motion as a non-confidence measure, turning an ordinary debate day into a potential government-toppling vote. This is uncommon but has precedent in Canadian parliamentary history.

More commonly, supply days surface issues the government would prefer to avoid debating: scandals, policy failures, or opposition priorities that never make it onto the government's legislative agenda. They are the opposition's best tool for forcing accountability on specific topics.
