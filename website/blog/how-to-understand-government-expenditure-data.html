How to Understand Government Expenditure Data

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Government spending in Canada goes through several layers of planning, authorization, and reporting. Understanding how these layers relate to each other is essential for interpreting what the numbers actually mean. Here is the framework.

The Budget: Fiscal Policy Intentions

The federal budget, presented by the Finance Minister each spring, announces the government's fiscal plan for the year: revenue projections, major new spending announcements, and the projected deficit or surplus. The budget is a policy document — it sets intentions but does not by itself authorize spending. Parliament must separately approve the actual expenditures.

The Estimates: What Parliament Actually Approves

Spending is authorized through "supply" — a series of Estimates documents tabled in Parliament.

  • Main Estimates — tabled in late February/early March, covering the bulk of planned departmental spending for the fiscal year (April 1 – March 31)
  • Supplementary Estimates A, B, C — tabled in spring, fall, and late winter to request additional funds for new initiatives, emergencies, or items not ready for the Main Estimates

Each Estimates document is studied by parliamentary committees. The Standing Committee on Government Operations and Estimates (OGGO) coordinates this review across departments. MPs can ask deputy ministers detailed questions about planned spending.

Public Accounts: What Was Actually Spent

The Public Accounts of Canada — tabled in the fall — report what was actually spent in the previous fiscal year. This is the authoritative comparison point: Main Estimates show what was planned; Public Accounts show what happened. Large variances are examined by the Standing Committee on Public Accounts (PAC) and can generate Auditor General reports.
