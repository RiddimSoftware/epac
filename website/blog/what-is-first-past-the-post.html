What is First Past the Post?

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Canada elects its federal Parliament using a voting system called First Past the Post (FPTP), also known as plurality voting. Understanding how it works explains some of the most counterintuitive outcomes in Canadian political history — like majority governments formed by parties that received less than 40% of votes.

How it Works

Canada is divided into 338 federal ridings. In each riding, voters cast a single ballot for their preferred candidate. The candidate who receives the most votes in that riding wins the seat — even if they received only 35% or 40% of the vote. There is no runoff, no transfer of preferences, no requirement to win a majority.

At the national level, the party that wins the most seats typically forms the government. A party needs 170 seats for a majority government; fewer seats means a minority government that must seek support from other parties to pass legislation.

Why Parties Can Win Majorities with Minority Vote Shares

FPTP can produce lopsided results when votes are geographically concentrated. A party that wins many ridings by narrow margins accumulates more seats than a party that wins fewer ridings by large margins. In recent Canadian elections, majority governments have been formed with national vote shares of 36–40%.

Conversely, a party with broad but shallow national support can win a significant share of votes but few seats — as the NDP has repeatedly experienced in ridings where it finishes second or third despite strong aggregate numbers.
