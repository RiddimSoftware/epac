What is the Throne Speech?

April 27, 2026 · 4 min read

At the start of each new Parliament, and sometimes at the start of a new session within the same Parliament, members of both chambers gather in the Senate chamber to hear the Speech from the Throne — a statement of the government's agenda for the coming session. Here is what it is, who delivers it, and why it matters.

Who Writes It and Who Reads It

Despite the name, the throne speech is written entirely by the Prime Minister's Office. It represents the government's policy agenda — what bills it intends to introduce, what priorities it is committing to. The Governor General reads it from the Senate throne, but the words are entirely the government's.

MPs and senators gather in the Senate to listen; the Commons does not meet in its own chamber for the ceremony. An older convention requires the Commons to symbolically "refuse" the speech by introducing a pro forma bill before responding — signalling that the elected chamber is not subordinate to the Crown.

Why it's a Confidence Matter

The House then debates the Address in Reply to the Speech from the Throne — typically for several days. A vote on the Address is a confidence vote: if the government loses, Parliament is dissolved or a new government is formed. In practice, majority governments rarely lose this vote; in minority situations, the government must be more careful about what it commits to in the speech.

What to Look for in Hansard

The debate on the Address in Reply is often the fullest statement of each party's position at the start of a Parliament. In epac, search the Hansard record for "Speech from the Throne" or "Address in Reply" to find these debates. They are a useful baseline for tracking whether the government followed through on its stated commitments.
