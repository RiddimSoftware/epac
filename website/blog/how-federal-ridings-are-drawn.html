How Federal Ridings Are Drawn in Canada

April 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Canada currently has 338 federal electoral districts — ridings — each electing one Member of Parliament. These boundaries are not permanent. After every decennial census, they are reviewed and often redrawn to reflect population changes. Here is how that process works and why it matters for democratic representation.

The Electoral Boundaries Readjustment Act

Federal riding boundaries are governed by the Electoral Boundaries Readjustment Act. After each census (conducted every 10 years), the Chief Electoral Officer triggers a redistribution process. Independent boundary commissions are established — one per province — composed of a judge, appointed by the chief justice of each province, and two other members appointed by the Speaker of the House.

Critically, these commissions operate independently of Parliament and political parties. Politicians cannot redraw boundaries for partisan advantage — unlike some other countries where redistricting is controlled by legislatures.

How Commissions Draw Boundaries

Each commission considers several factors when drawing boundaries:

  • Population equality — the primary criterion; ridings should have roughly equal populations (within ±25% of the provincial average)
  • Communities of interest — ridings should, where possible, respect community ties, municipal boundaries, and historical boundaries
  • Geographic features — particularly in rural and northern areas where vast geographic coverage may justify lower population

Commissions hold public hearings and accept written submissions. MPs can also make representations to the commissions — but they cannot vote on or override the recommendations. Parliament can only delay implementation, not change the final maps.

Why Riding Names and Boundaries Change

After a redistribution, some ridings are renamed, new ones are created, and old ones are merged or split. An MP elected in one riding may find their constituency looks quite different after redistribution — possibly favouring a different party's base. This is the intended effect of an independent process: boundaries reflect population, not electoral convenience.
