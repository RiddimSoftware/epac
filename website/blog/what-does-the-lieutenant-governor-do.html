What Does the Lieutenant Governor Do?

April 27, 2026 · 3 min read

Canada's federal system has two levels of representative monarchy. At the federal level, the Governor General represents the Crown. In each of Canada's ten provinces, the same role is performed by a Lieutenant Governor (often written as Lt. Gov.). Here is what the Lieutenant Governor does and how the role works.

The Crown's Representative in Each Province

A Lieutenant Governor is appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister (not the provincial Premier). This creates an interesting dynamic: the LG is a federal appointment, but their role is entirely provincial. They represent the Crown — and ultimately the constitutional guardrails of the parliamentary system — within their province.

What the Lieutenant Governor Does

The LG's formal duties mirror the Governor General's at the provincial level:

  • Swearing in the Premier and cabinet — When a new government forms after an election, the LG administers the oaths of office
  • Granting Royal Assent to provincial legislation — Provincial bills must receive the LG's assent to become law, just as federal bills require the Governor General's assent
  • Summoning and proroguing the provincial legislature — On the advice of the Premier
  • Dissolving the legislature and calling elections — On the Premier's advice or, in rare circumstances, at the LG's own discretion

The Reserve Powers

Like the Governor General, the Lieutenant Governor holds reserve powers that can theoretically override convention — they could refuse to grant Royal Assent to a bill, refuse to dissolve the legislature, or dismiss a Premier. In practice these powers are almost never invoked, but their existence provides a constitutional backstop against legislative overreach.

The most significant recent use of provincial viceregal discretion was in 2017 in British Columbia, where LG Judith Guichon navigated a minority government situation requiring careful constitutional judgment.
